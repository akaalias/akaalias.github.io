Bookmarked from conor brennan-burke @contextconor

one of the strangest things about moving from new york to sf was realizing how often extremely important things happen here in settings that don't feel important at all

i lived in new york for four years. i sometimes spent entire days moving icons around in powerpoint, but i was doing it in a skyscraper with great views, surrounded by attractive people in patagonia vests. afterward i'd walk onto a packed street and go to a beautiful restaurant

even when the work itself was mundane, everything around it told you it mattered

sf is almost the opposite

the blue bottle in south park feels like a normal coffee shop. it is also a place where an absurd amount of capital gets allocated

sand hill road is one of the most powerful financial centers in the world and looks like a collection of dentist offices

you'll meet someone dressed like they just rolled out of bed who can't make eye contact and discover later that they control billions of dollars or have built a product you use every day

i've been to parties where almost everyone had raised millions of dollars or built products used by millions of people. in another city, each person would have seemed like a big deal. in sf, they were just standing around a cramped apartment at a "party" that honestly wasn't even that good

i used to live in a closet in a slightly dilapidated hacker house with creaky floors and old furniture. people who lived there went on to create companies worth tens of billions of dollars

new york puts a lot of effort into making importance visible. sf often seems actively uninterested in doing that

some of this is probably because sf is more focused on creation than consumption. some of it is the personality of builders, who often care much more about the thing they're making than the room they're making it in

but i think the lack of polish also lowers the barrier to entry. you don't need to look important before doing something important here

new york makes ordinary work feel important

sf makes important work feel ordinary

I found a previously undocumented passive Windows backdoor I’m calling SLEEPWALKER. Its defining feature is a custom command language with 23 instructions. Instead of receiving conventional commands, it runs encrypted bytecode programs that can combine scheduling, nested programs, staged payload delivery with SHA-256 verification, decompression, network communication and in-memory shellcode execution.

The analyzed file is an unsigned DLL that masquerades as Microsoft's dpapi.dll, uses forged ESET metadata and activates only when side-loaded by ESET's Management Agent program ERAAgent.exe. SLEEPWALKER does not beacon or open an obvious listening port on its own. It monitors network traffic and stays dormant until it detects a specially crafted packet carrying an AES-256-CCM-protected task. It supports TCP, UDP, ICMP, SMB named pipes and VMware VMCI. VMCI can carry traffic directly between a VMware guest and host without using a conventional network interface. SLEEPWALKER also implements a DNS trigger that uses Base32 to carry encrypted tasks inside DNS labels, although the analyzed sample does not enable it.

The full analysis documents the bytecode format, all 23 instructions, encryption, DNS encoding, trigger logic and network transports. It also includes IOCs, a YARA rule and a PowerShell scanner.

If you believe you have been targeted by SLEEPWALKER or have found a related sample, please contact me. I created the SLEEPWALKER Analysis Toolkit to decode its bytecode, examine artifacts and safely reproduce its receiving pipeline without executing commands or transmitting data. If you suspect you've been infected, I am happy to share this toolkit with you, alongside a mitigation guide providing investigation steps and a remediation script for use after detection.
